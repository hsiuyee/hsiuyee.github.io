# Intro

Have you ever encountered the following situation?

One day, you have a conflict with one of your colleagues, friends, or partners. So, you try to solve the conflict between you and the other person. Although you have tried your best to communicate with them, the relationship has worsened, leading to further conflicts.

Therefore, you may feel frustrated since your goals and needs are not being met. Naturally, you want to explain yourself in detail because you feel that the other person does not fully understand what you mean, but this may come across as an attack. On the other hand, the other person feels the same way, so they also explain themselves more, which comes across as defensive. In the end, both of you fall into the well-known [criticism–defensiveness cycle](https://www.gottman.com/blog/criticism-defensiveness-cycle/).

Have you ever thought about how to avoid or break out of this cycle?

In today’s blog, I wanna share with you a useful technique called nonviolent communication (NVC) to resolve conflicts and truly understand each other.

# Nonviolent Communication (NVC)

Nonviolent Communication is a language proposed by Marshall B. Rosenberg, Ph.D., in the book [Nonviolent Communication](https://www.amazon.com/dp/189200528X?lv=shuf&channelId=500&plpRedirect=mhFallback), which aims to minimize violent reactions such as defending, withdrawing, or attacking.

This method has four steps:

1. **observations**: observe without introducing any judgment or evaluation
2. **feelings**: state how we feel when we observe this action
3. **needs**: find which of our needs are connected to the feelings we have identified in the previous step
4. **requests**: say what we want from the other person that would enrich our lives or make life more wonderful for us

# Difficulties of NVC

Although it seems easy at first glance, the first step is quite difficult. If you are not convinced, you can try to find which of the following statements are observations without evaluation.

1. “John was angry with me yesterday for no reason.”
2. “Yesterday evening, Nancy bit her fingernails while watching television.”
3. “Sam didn’t ask for my opinion during the meeting.”
4. “My father is a good man.”
5. “Janice works too much.”
6. “Henry is aggressive.”
7. “Pam was first in line every day this week.”
8. “My son often doesn’t brush his teeth.”
9. “Luke told me I didn’t look good in yellow.”
10. “My aunt complains when I talk with her.”

The answer is only 2, 3, 7, and 9, which means that most of our statements include subjective evaluations or inferences.

Second, because we may be terrified of sharing our fears with others, some of these steps can be hard to achieve. Third, we may miss some steps. For example, we may simply describe our observations but forget to make a specific request, leaving both sides dissatisfied.

For example, I may be asked to look carefully at maps or pay attention to the fire while eating hot pot. You may think that her need is to be cared for. So, from my point of view, I turn that need into requests: remembering what she said in detail, such as when she has her period, or giving her her favorite snacks. However, her actual need may be shared responsibility, which means that my actions and assumptions may be completely wrong.

I feel frustrated because I have tried many ways to improve the relationship. The other person is also disappointed because she has told me about her needs many times. In the end, the conflict seems inevitable.

# Conclusion

Both speakers and listeners have to practice nonviolent communication to resolve conflicts. Even if the conversation starts to go in the wrong direction, we should identify the four components. This means that instead of reacting automatically with System 1, we need to reflect using System 2 (see [Thinking, Fast and Slow](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0374533555?lv=shuf&channelId=500&plpRedirect=mhFallback)).

In this way, we will be less likely to damage the relationship.
